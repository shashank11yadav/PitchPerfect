## Why it matters

Buyers rarely open with their real problem. Ask “how are things going?” and you’ll hear “fine, mostly.” The pain is in there somewhere, but you have to dig for it, and you get one shot before they get tired of answering questions. Good discovery is why the pitch later on sounds like it was written for them.

By the end of a discovery call you should be able to say what’s wrong, what it costs them, and who else gets a say in fixing it.

## A simple framework

Work down in layers, one question at a time.

1. **Situation.** How things work today. “Walk me through what happens when a new order comes in.”
2. **Problem.** Where it breaks. “Where does that usually go sideways?”
3. **Impact.** What the problem costs in time, money or stress. “When that happens, what does it cost you?”
4. **Decision.** How a change would get made. “If you wanted to fix this, who else would weigh in?”

When an answer sounds interesting, go one level deeper before you move on. “What happens then?” is the most useful question in sales.

## Example

Sam is calling Lena, who manages a busy veterinary clinic.

**Lena:** Things are fine, mostly. Some scheduling stuff.

**Sam:** What does “scheduling stuff” look like on a bad day?

**Lena:** Two vets double-booked, someone waiting forty minutes with an anxious dog.

**Sam:** How often does that happen?

**Lena:** Once or twice a week. We had a one-star review about it last month.

**Sam:** What happened after the review?

**Lena:** The owner asked me to fix it by January.

**Sam:** Who else would weigh in on picking a fix?

**Lena:** Me and the owner. Maybe our head tech, since she builds the schedule.

The question that did the most work was “what happened after the review?” It took Lena from an annoyance to a deadline with her boss’s name on it. Sam still hasn’t mentioned his product, and he doesn’t need to yet.

## Common mistakes

- Yes-or-no questions. “Is scheduling a problem?” gets you “kind of.” Start with how, what, or “tell me about.”
- Stacking questions. Ask three at once and they’ll answer the easiest one.
- Pitching at the first sign of pain. The first problem you hear is usually the shallow one, so stay curious a little longer.
- Interrogating. React to what they say before you ask the next thing. “Forty minutes, that’s rough” goes a long way.

## Practice prompt

The practice partner plays a vague buyer who won’t volunteer much. Your goal: by the end, you can write down the problem, what it costs, and who decides, using only what the buyer told you. Discovery questions carry double weight on this scorecard, and the partner rewards open questions that build on the last answer far more than a long list of unrelated ones.

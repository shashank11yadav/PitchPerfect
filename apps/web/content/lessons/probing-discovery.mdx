## Why it matters

Buyers rarely open with their real problem. Ask “how are things going?” and you’ll hear “fine, mostly.” The pain is in there somewhere, but you have to dig for it, and you only get one shot before they get tired of answering questions. Good discovery is why the pitch later on sounds like it was written for them.

By the end of a discovery call you should be able to say three things: what’s wrong, what it costs them, and who else gets a say in fixing it.

## A simple framework

Work down in layers, one question at a time.

1. **Situation.** How things work today. “Walk me through what happens when a new order comes in.”
2. **Problem.** Where it breaks. “Where does that usually go sideways?”
3. **Impact.** What the problem costs, in time, money or stress. “When that happens, what does it cost you?”
4. **Decision.** How a change would get made. “If you wanted to fix this, who else would weigh in?”

When an answer sounds interesting, go one level deeper before you move on. “What happens then?” is the most useful question in sales.

## Example

Sam is calling Lena, who manages a busy veterinary clinic.

**Lena:** Things are fine, mostly. Some scheduling stuff.

**Sam:** What does “scheduling stuff” look like on a bad day?

**Lena:** Two vets double-booked, someone waiting forty minutes with an anxious dog.

**Sam:** How often does that happen?

**Lena:** Once or twice a week. We had a one-star review about it last month.

**Sam:** What happened after the review?

**Lena:** The owner asked me to fix it by January.

**Sam:** So you’d be the one picking a fix. Would the owner sign off on it, or is that your call?

Four questions in, Sam knows the pain (double bookings), the cost (bad reviews, angry owner), the deadline (January) and who decides. He hasn’t pitched a thing yet.

## Common mistakes

- **Yes-or-no questions.** “Is scheduling a problem?” gets you “kind of.” Ask how, what or tell me about.
- **Stacking questions.** Ask three at once and they’ll answer the easiest one.
- **Pitching at the first sign of pain.** The first problem you hear is usually the shallow one. Stay curious a little longer.
- **Interrogating.** React to what they say before you fire the next question. “Forty minutes, that’s rough” goes a long way.

## Practice prompt

The practice partner plays a vague buyer who won’t volunteer much. Your goal: by the end, write down the problem, what it costs, and who decides, using only what the buyer told you. Discovery questions count double, and the scorecard rewards open questions that build on the last answer.

## Why it matters

At a career fair or a networking night you get about thirty seconds before the other person decides whether to keep listening or start looking over your shoulder. Most students spend those seconds reading out their resume in order: school, major, year, clubs. The recruiter has heard that from forty people today. What sticks is one clear point about you and a reason to keep talking.

## A simple framework

Keep it to three sentences, and then let the other person talk.

1. **Who you are, in plain words.** Your name and the one thing you’re doing right now.
2. **One piece of proof.** A number that shows a result. “I made 60 calls a week” says you were busy. “I booked 14 meetings from those calls” says you were good at it.
3. **Why them, and a question.** Something specific about their company, then a question that hands the conversation back.

The question does more work than it looks like. Whatever they answer tells you what this person cares about, and that’s what you talk about next.

## Example

Alicia meets Marcus, a sales manager at a payroll software company that sells to restaurants, at a regional meetup.

**Marcus:** So what brings you here tonight?

**Alicia:** I’m Alicia, and I graduate in May with a business degree after two summers selling kitchen remodels door to door. I knocked on about thirty doors a night and closed roughly one in twenty, which put me second on a crew of twelve. Your team sells to restaurant owners, the same people who made up half my doors, so I’m curious what your new reps spend their first month on.

**Marcus:** Mostly listening to calls and learning the product. One in twenty at the door, though? How’d you get people to open up?

Marcus skipped her degree and went straight for the number. That’s the whole job of the proof sentence: give them something specific enough to ask about.

## Common mistakes

Reading the resume aloud is the big one. Pick one thing and trust that the rest can come up once they’re interested.

Hedging is the next. “I’m kind of thinking about maybe sales?” sounds like you aren’t. Say “I want to start in sales.”

Watch where your best detail sits, too. If it lands at second fifty, you lost them at twenty. And if you end without a question, they have to carry the conversation, and most people won’t bother.

## Practice prompt

Write your three sentences down, read them out loud, and time it. Anything past 45 seconds needs cutting. Then run it with the practice partner, who plays a hiring manager or a prospect you’ve just met at an event. The partner scores clarity at double weight for this skill, so if your main point got buried, the scorecard will quote the sentence where it happened.

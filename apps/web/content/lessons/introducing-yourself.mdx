## Why it matters

At a career fair or a networking night you get about thirty seconds before the other person decides whether to keep listening or start looking over your shoulder. Most students spend those seconds reading out their resume in order: school, major, year, clubs. The recruiter has heard that from forty people today. What sticks is one clear point about you and a reason to keep talking.

## A simple framework

Three sentences, then stop.

1. **Who you are, in plain words.** Your name and the one thing you’re doing right now. “I’m Jordan. I’m a senior in marketing, and for the last year I’ve run outreach for our campus food bank.”
2. **One piece of proof.** A number or a small story that shows you can do the work. “We went from 12 business donors to 41, mostly by cold calling.”
3. **Why them, then a question.** “Your team is hiring SDRs in Denver. What does the first ninety days look like in that seat?”

The question does more work than it looks like. It hands the conversation back, and the answer tells you what this person cares about.

## Example

Alicia meets Marcus, a sales manager at a payroll software company, at a regional meetup.

**Marcus:** So what brings you here tonight?

**Alicia:** I’m Alicia. I graduate in May with a business degree, and the last two summers I sold kitchen remodels door to door, about thirty doors a night. I want a phone-based sales job where I’m not standing in the rain. What do your new reps spend most of their first month on?

**Marcus:** Mostly listening to calls and learning the product. Door to door, though. What did that teach you?

Look at what happened. Marcus grabbed the one specific detail and asked about it. That’s the proof sentence doing its job, and now Alicia gets to tell a story instead of reciting a list.

## Common mistakes

- **Reading the resume aloud.** Pick one thing. The rest can come up once they’re interested.
- **Hedging.** “I’m kind of thinking about maybe sales?” sounds like you aren’t. Say “I want to start in sales.”
- **Saving the best part for last.** If your strongest detail lands at second fifty, you lost them at twenty.
- **Ending without a question.** Then they have to carry the conversation, and most people won’t bother.

## Practice prompt

Write your three sentences down, read them out loud, and time it. If it runs past 45 seconds, cut words until it doesn’t. Then run it with the practice partner, who plays a hiring manager or a prospect you’ve just met at an event. Clarity counts double on this skill, so the scorecard will tell you plainly whether your main point came through or got buried.
